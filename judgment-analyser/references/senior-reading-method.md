# The Senior Reading Method

Load this during Step 3. This is the difference between a summary and an analysis.

---

## The Sequence

A junior reads a judgment in the order it was written. A senior reads it in the order
that reveals what it decided.

**1. Disposition first.**
Go to the last few paragraphs. What did the court actually do? Allowed, dismissed,
partly allowed, remanded, set aside, quashed, directions issued, matter referred to a
larger bench? Costs? Any operative directions with timelines? Write this down before
reading anything else.

**2. Issues the court framed for itself.**
Most Indian judgments state the questions for determination somewhere in the first
third — often numbered, often introduced by "the following questions arise" or "the
short question is". Note the court's own framing. It is more reliable than the
issues the parties framed, and it controls what the ratio can be.

**3. Work backwards from disposition to necessity.**
For each issue: was deciding it *necessary* to reach the disposition? If the appeal
was dismissed on limitation, everything the court said on merits is obiter, however
long and however confident. This is the single most common error in junior case notes.

**4. What the court did not decide.**
Look for "we express no opinion", "it is unnecessary to decide", "we leave the
question open", "in the facts of this case". These phrases are the court fencing off
its own holding, and they are where the next argument lives. A senior notes them; a
junior skips them.

**5. Facts — but only the material ones.**
Read the facts *after* you know the holding, so you can tell which facts the court
actually relied on. A fact the court repeats in its reasoning is material. A fact
recited once in the background and never mentioned again is not. Material facts
define how far the ratio travels.

**6. Arguments last.**
Knowing the outcome tells you which arguments the court engaged with and which it
recorded and ignored. An argument that is set out at length and then never addressed
is a signal — either the court found it unnecessary, or it is a gap. Note which.

## What a Senior Notices That a Junior Misses

- **The standard of review.** Was the court sitting in appeal, in revision, in writ,
  in a second appeal under s.100 CPC, in an SLP? This limits what the court could
  decide and therefore what it did decide. A finding of fact disturbed in a writ
  petition means something different from the same finding in a first appeal.

- **Concessions.** "Learned counsel fairly conceded" quietly removes an issue from
  contest. A holding built on a concession is weak authority for a contested case.

- **Findings recorded vs findings affirmed.** A court reciting the tribunal's finding
  is not making that finding. Watch the verbs.

- **Separate opinions.** A concurrence that reaches the same result by different
  reasoning narrows the ratio to what the judges actually agreed on. A dissent is not
  binding but is frequently the best available statement of the counter-argument.

- **The judgment's own citations.** Which authorities did the court follow, which did
  it distinguish, and which did it merely refer to? A judgment that distinguishes a
  binding precedent rather than following it is doing something significant.

- **Concluding directions.** Indian judgments often close with directions to
  authorities, timelines, or guidelines to operate until legislation is enacted.
  These are operative and enforceable even when analytically obiter. Flag them
  separately — their practical force does not match their doctrinal classification.

- **Length as a signal.** Where a court spends thirty paragraphs on one point and two
  on another, the two-paragraph point may be the one it was least comfortable with.

## Reading for a Purpose

Step 2 captures why the judgment is being read. Adjust accordingly:

| Purpose | Foreground |
|---|---|
| Supporting a position | The narrowest formulation of the ratio that still helps, and the material facts that must be matched |
| Resisting reliance | Distinguishing facts, obiter mislabelled as ratio, concessions, per incuriam and sub silentio arguments, what the court left open |
| Advising a client | The operative directions, what changes in practice, timelines, and what remains unsettled |
| Understanding the law | The doctrinal position before and after, and where the judgment sits in the line of authority |
| Content or teaching | The one clean proposition a non-litigator would take away, plus the caveat that keeps it honest |

State in the case note which lens was applied. The same judgment reads differently
depending on why it is being read, and the reader deserves to know which reading
they are getting.
