# Ratio Decidendi and Obiter Dicta

Load this during Step 4. This is the section that makes the output worth more than a
summary.

---

## The Working Definition

The **ratio decidendi** is the proposition of law applied to the material facts that
was **necessary** to the court's disposition. Everything else the court said is
**obiter dicta** — however emphatic, however lengthy, however useful.

Only the ratio binds. Obiter persuades.

---

## Test 1: The Necessity Test (primary)

For each proposition of law in the judgment, ask:

> If the court had decided this point the other way, would the outcome have changed?

- **Yes** → capable of being ratio.
- **No** → obiter.

Worked application: an appeal is dismissed on the ground of limitation. The court
then spends fifteen paragraphs holding that the contract clause was unconscionable.
That holding is obiter — reversing it would not have revived the time-barred appeal.
Fifteen paragraphs of obiter is still obiter.

## Test 2: The Material Facts Test (scope)

The ratio is tied to the facts the court treated as material. Restate it as:

> In a case where [material facts], the law is [proposition].

Strip out immaterial facts and the ratio broadens; add facts back and it narrows.
State the ratio at the level of generality the court itself used. Do not widen it to
make it more useful, and do not narrow it to make it easier to distinguish. If two
formulations are both defensible, record both and mark the proposition CONTESTABLE.

## Test 3: The Alternative Holdings Problem

Where a court gives two independent grounds for the same result ("in any event, even
if we are wrong on the first point..."), Indian courts have generally treated both as
binding rather than treating the second as obiter. Record both as ratio, and note
that the alternative-holding character gives an opponent a distinguishing argument.

## Test 4: Multi-Judge Benches

- **Unanimous single opinion** — ratio is straightforward.
- **Concurring opinions with different reasoning** — the binding ratio is the
  narrowest ground on which a majority agreed. Reasoning in a concurrence that no
  other judge joined is not ratio. Identify the common ground expressly.
- **Plurality with no majority reasoning** — say so. There may be no binding ratio on
  that point at all, only a binding result.
- **Dissent** — never ratio. Record it separately; it is the best-articulated version
  of the counter-argument and worth having.

## Common Misclassifications to Guard Against

| Looks like ratio | Actually |
|---|---|
| A long, quotable statement of general principle | Usually obiter unless the disposition turned on it |
| Guidelines issued for future cases | Obiter in doctrine, but operative in practice — flag both |
| The court's summary of an earlier case's holding | Not this court's ratio; it is a restatement, and may be inaccurate |
| A proposition the parties conceded | Not decided, therefore not ratio |
| Findings under Article 142 | Expressly not precedent in most such orders |
| The headnote's bolded proposition | An editor's view, not the court's |
| Answer to a question referred to a larger bench | This IS ratio, even if the bench then remits the matter |

---

## Obiter Worth Recording Anyway

Do not discard obiter. Classify it, then record the parts that matter:

- **Obiter of the Supreme Court** is highly persuasive on all courts and routinely
  followed. Note this expressly — telling a reader "this is only obiter" without
  adding "and Supreme Court obiter is followed in practice" is technically correct
  and practically misleading.
- **Considered obiter** — where the court heard full argument on the point and
  decided it deliberately though unnecessarily — carries more weight than a passing
  remark.
- **Judicial signalling** — "the legislature may wish to consider", "we are
  constrained by the present state of the law" — tells you where the law is moving.
